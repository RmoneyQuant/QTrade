An adapter is a protocol, not an exchange

The distinction matters because Quincy delivers CME and DGCX over one normalised feed. Draw one adapter per exchange and you have already got the model wrong.

Time is a scheduler, not a counter

A clock advanced only by market data cannot detect the absence of market data — and that absence is exactly what a staleness timeout or a missed heartbeat is. Market data is one event source among eight.

MBO and MBP are not interchangeable

MCX gives order-by-order data, so exact queue position is computable. Quincy gives aggregated depth, where it simply is not. Flatten them into one book type and a strategy relying on queue position works on MCX and quietly degrades to guesswork on the signal venues.

The Simulated Exchange sees nothing of yours

In production the venue is not inside your process. If the simulator read your Cache you would create a coupling that does not exist live — and one with a specific failure mode.

Your feed-derived book can go STALE after a packet gap. A real exchange does not become uncertain because your receiver dropped packets. A simulator reading your Cache would inherit your corruption and fill you against a book that never existed.

Why the simulator cannot fill you at T

Between the exchange stamping an event and your order reaching the matching engine, other participants have already acted. Recorded MTBT lets you estimate feed latency; outbound latency is not observable from market data at all and stays a parameter until real round-trips exist.

Two recording points, and neither substitutes for the other

The Journal cannot be derived from per-stream captures, because live consumes in arrival order and a timestamp merge may legitimately produce a different interleaving. It has to be recorded where the merge happens.

Configuration in, results out

Figure 1 shows the data and order paths. This is the other half: what defines a run, and what a run produces. Both travel over the Control Dispatcher — the low-rate counterpart to the Event Dispatcher, where observers register by wiring rather than by anyone publishing to them.

The split between the two config files matters more than it looks. Run identity is (config hash, build hash). If multicast addresses lived in the hashed config, moving to a different colocation rack would change your run hash and make every previous result appear incomparable — wrong, since an endpoint has no bearing on outcomes.

What keeps the modes identical

Shared code is necessary but not sufficient — shared code still diverges if it can reach ambient state. These constraints are what actually enforce parity, and they are cheapest to adopt before the first strategy exists.

Injected time

Nothing in the qtrade reads a clock

No system clock, no random(), no direct I/O. Time arrives only from the Scheduler. Monotonic for ordering, wall clock for meaning — so an NTP step cannot silently reorder events.

Total ordering

Every tie has a defined winner

Ordered by (timestamp, class, seq), the sequence assigned at enqueue. Without it the same backtest run twice gives different answers — a correctness bug wearing a performance costume.

Shared decoder

One decoder per protocol, both paths

Live and replay converge before decoding, so a decoder defect reproduces in backtest instead of waiting for production.

Same source

Session state per venue, not per mode

MCX publishes its state and recordings preserve it, so backtest reads the same messages live does. A calendar-in-backtest split would mean unscheduled halts never appear in a backtest.

Venue isolation

The simulator reads nothing of yours

Commands in, reports out, its own books. Identical interface to the live gateway, so the ExecutionEngine cannot tell them apart — and your feed gaps cannot corrupt it.

Explicit staleness

An uncertain book cannot quote

UNINIT and RECOVERING block quoting outright, enforced by the watchdog rather than by strategy convention — because the moment you most need quotes pulled is the moment the strategy is misbehaving.

Proving the simulator, not trusting it

The Simulated Exchange is the component that can lie silently. A wrong book crashes or visibly diverges; a wrong fill model produces plausible numbers that get trusted for months and then bet on.

Is one-tick market making viable at all?

No market-maker fee concession applies, so round-trip cost — exchange charges, SEBI fees, CTT on the sell leg, GST, stamp duty on the buy leg — is a hard floor on the quotable spread. Measuring that floor against the historical MCX Crude spread distribution needs no engine, and it either validates the phase-1 premise or redirects it while redirecting is still cheap.
